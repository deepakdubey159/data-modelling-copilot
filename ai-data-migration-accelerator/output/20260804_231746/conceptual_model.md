# Executive Summary

This database supports a direct-to-customer retail business that sells physical goods from a catalogue, holds them across multiple warehouses, and fulfils customer orders through shipment, payment and returns. Six subject areas are visible: customers and their orders, the product catalogue and its suppliers, warehouse stock and fulfilment, the internal workforce that services accounts, a shared geographic hierarchy, and a change-tracking log. The commercial core is the sales order, which connects a customer, a salesperson, one or more catalogue products, a payment, a shipment from a warehouse, and any subsequent return.

> This conceptual model was generated by **claude-opus-5 (in-session; generated without an API call)** from the deterministic metadata, profile and relationship artifacts. It is an interpretation of the business the database appears to support, and should be reviewed by someone who knows that business. The Assumptions section lists where interpretation happened.

# Business Domains

## Customer and Sales

The demand side of the business: who buys, what they ordered, and how they paid. This is the commercial heart of the model and the most heavily connected part of the schema.

**Entities:** Customer, Sales Order, Order Line, Payment

## Product and Sourcing

The catalogue of sellable goods, how it is organised for browsing, who supplies it, and the promotional offers applied to it.

**Entities:** Product, Product Category, Supplier, Promotion

## Inventory and Fulfilment

Where goods are physically held and how they reach the customer or come back. Covers stock positions per location, outbound shipments, and returned goods.

**Entities:** Warehouse, Stock Level, Shipment, Product Return

## Workforce

The internal organisation. Employees are servicing accounts as sales representatives and are arranged in a reporting hierarchy within departments.

**Entities:** Employee, Department

## Geography

A shared three-level location hierarchy used as reference data by both customers and warehouses. It is not owned by any one business process.

**Entities:** City, State, Country

## Governance

Change tracking. Records that a data-modifying operation occurred, who performed it and when.

**Entities:** Audit Entry

# Business Rules

1. Every order line must belong to an order and is identified by its position within it; a line cannot exist independently of its order.
2. An order records both the customer who placed it and the employee who handled it, so sales performance can be attributed to an individual.
3. The price charged is captured on the order line as well as on the product, so later catalogue price changes do not alter the value of historical orders.
4. Customers are granted a credit limit, indicating that goods are supplied on account and that exposure per customer is controlled.
5. Stock is tracked per product per warehouse, and each stock position carries a reorder level, implying an automatic replenishment trigger.
6. Order status, payment method, product status, customer status and country code are all drawn from short controlled vocabularies rather than free text.
7. A product may be sourced from only one supplier at a time, so alternative sourcing is not supported by the current model.
8. Both customers and warehouses are located through the same city, state and country hierarchy, so a single geography is shared across the business.
9. Employees form a single reporting hierarchy in which exactly one person has no manager.
10. Payment, shipment and return are each recorded separately from the order, so an order may exist unpaid, undispatched, or without any return.

# Assumptions

1. The employee attributed to an order is a sales representative. The reference is named for the role rather than for the entity it points at, so this reading is inferred from the role name and not from the structure.
2. The inventory record is treated as a business entity, Stock Level, rather than a simple association, because it carries quantity and reorder level -- measures the business acts on.
3. The product-to-promotion association is treated as a pure relationship and not an entity, because it carries no attributes of its own.
4. Every table in the analysis contains exactly ten rows, and several columns hold a single repeated value such as one order status and one payment method. This strongly suggests seeded demonstration data. Value distributions were therefore used to identify controlled vocabularies but not to draw conclusions about real business volumes or mix.
5. The audit record refers to affected records by name in a text column rather than through a relationship, so it has been modelled as standing alone. If it is intended to reference specific rows, that intent is not expressed in the structure.
6. Country is assumed to be the top of the hierarchy even though the sample data shows every state assigned to a single country; this is read as a property of the sample rather than a business rule.
7. Order status was read as a fulfilment lifecycle. Only one value is present in the sample, so the full set of permitted states cannot be determined from the data alone.

# Recommendations

1. No entity represents a postal or delivery address. Location is captured only to city level, which is insufficient to actually deliver an order and should be confirmed with the business.
2. Promotion carries no start or end date, so it is not possible to determine when an offer was active or which orders it should have applied to.
3. The order carries no total value. Whether order value is derived on demand from its lines or is expected to be held should be settled before reporting is built on this model.
4. A return records a reason and the product but neither a quantity nor a refunded amount, so returns cannot currently be reconciled against payments.
5. Shipment records a tracking number but no carrier, so tracking references cannot be resolved without knowing who carried the goods.
6. Payment is modelled as many per order, but there is no allocation of a payment across order lines. Part-payment and instalment handling should be clarified.
7. The audit entry is disconnected from everything it describes. If audit history needs to be queried alongside business data, a modelled relationship is required.
8. Confirm whether the workforce data belongs in this model at all. Employee salary sits alongside customer and order data with no separation, which is usually a concern for access control.
